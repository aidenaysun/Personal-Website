FTC Robotics

2022–2026 — autonomous control and mechanism design

Odometry-Based Autonomous Pathing

Used in the 2023–2025 seasons

Our early autonomous used timed, stop-and-go position drives that were slow and imprecise. I replaced them by logging the robot's global pose (x, y, θ) from odometry and IMU data, plotting those runs, and generating optimized paths from the plots. A continuous drive-to-position controller then follows the path, which cut runtime and improved precision.

Tools: Android Studio, Java, OpenCV, TensorFlow.

Autonomous Configuration Menu

2023 season, reused in 2024–2025

In the 2023 season our autonomous had to coordinate with our alliance partner's in a shared space, so parameters like start and truss delays, park location, detected color and which program to run had to be adjustable before each match.

I built a menu interface as a finite state machine that cycles through six entries with bounded wrap-around, using a pointer to the current entry, modular indexing and switch cases for context-sensitive control. It let us adapt quickly to field conditions and partner strategies, was reused in the 2024–2025 season, and helped our team reach the FTC State Championship.

Intake and Claw Design

2023–2024 — Autodesk Fusion

One challenge of the 2023–2024 FTC season was to collect pyramid-shaped game elements arranged in random orientations. Our robot began with a passive intake, but it had a weak grip when clipping and was inconsistent with pieces in arbitrary orientations.

I designed a custom claw: a gearbox-driven actuator with induced-fit, pyramid-shaped fingers optimized for form-fit contact, mounted on a servo-driven mechanism that provides three degrees of freedom (rotation about the x, y and z axes) to orient the claw. The geometry improved contact reliability and scoring efficiency and was integrated into the final robot.

Flywheel Shooter and Spindexer

Autodesk Fusion

For a game that required shooting plastic ball artifacts up a high chute, I designed a shooter system with:

  • A flywheel to launch the ball, balancing speed and accuracy by keeping contact for a controlled acceleration.
  • A "flapper" mechanism that adjusts the launch angle through a linkage and an encoder-equipped Axon servo.
  • A compact 12-inch "spindexer" with three custom walls and an integrated kicker that indexes balls and feeds the shooter, within the 18-inch size limit, with space reserved for color sensors for automatic sorting.

Flywheel sizing

I modeled the flywheel as a solid disk: moment of inertia I = ½mr², stored energy E = ¼mr²ω², matched to the energy needed to bring the ball to its target speed (½mbvb²). That let me choose mass and radius within the geometric constraints, and I verified the radius by modeling the ball's path along the ramp in Fusion 360.

The modular design includes mounting points for a lazy-susan turret for more accurate shooting. Fusion 360 motion studies of the full assembly (intake, spindexer, shooter, flapper and turret) confirmed kinematic clearances and mechanical stability.

Torque and Motor Selection

Hardware analysis

When selecting linear actuator motors, I applied physics and calculus to compute the minimum required torque instead of relying on trial and error.

Given subsystem mass m and required acceleration a, F = ma. For a lead screw of effective radius rs and mechanical efficiency η < 1, the torque requirement is τ = m·a·rs / η.

I computed efficiency curves and matched gearbox ratios to find the best torque-speed balance, settling on a 435 RPM motor that minimizes losses and heat.
