ML-Enhanced Spectral Sensing

Co-authored with Brody Aysun and Colin Aysun — Jun. 2025–Oct. 2025

Machine Learning-Enhanced Spectral Sensing for Advanced Color Discrimination Under Varying Ambience

Our team's autonomous color detection failed under inconsistent lighting at an FTC League Championship. We set out to build a color detection system that holds up when the lighting changes, using a low-cost 10-channel AS7341 spectral sensor on an Arduino Nano.

Approach

  • Collected normalized F1–F8 channel readings (415–680 nm) for five paint colors under four lighting conditions, calibrated against a dark reference and white PTFE tape.
  • Baseline: cosine similarity against pre-calibrated reference spectra, converted to probabilities with a softmax. The temperature was tuned by minimizing negative log-likelihood (T ≈ 0.09).
  • Machine learning: a 100-tree Random Forest classifier (scikit-learn, 80/20 split) trained on the raw normalized channels, learning illumination-independent patterns without any illuminant correction.
  • Compared both systems with calibrated probabilities, box plots per lighting condition, RMS error, and a Welch's t-test.

Results

  • Average accuracy of 0.86 for the Random Forest versus 0.43 for cosine similarity.
  • Roughly an 87% reduction in mean squared error relative to the baseline (RMS 0.19 versus 0.57); Welch's t-test gave p < 0.001.
  • Yellow light was the hardest condition, with some confusion between yellow and orange.

My role

The paper's roles were deliberately loose, with the work shared almost evenly. My primary responsibility was hardware and CAD: soldering the sensor, wiring it securely to the Arduino Nano, and designing the 3D-printed shroud (Fusion 360, PrusaSlicer) that blocks ambient light for the LED condition. I also designed the Arduino firmware to log and plot all 10 channels, and worked on the softmax conversion that let us compare cosine similarity with the Random Forest's probabilities.
